# House vinyl — names, words, and what has to be on it

A word bank and a checklist. Pick, mix, change. Nothing here is a concept —
it is raw material.

---

## Label names

Short, flat, functional. House labels almost never sound poetic.

| | |
|---|---|
| SECONDS | PLAIN AUDIO |
| LOW CEILING | UNIT 7 |
| SUBLEVEL | NIGHT SHIFT |
| HOLD MUSIC | THIRD FLOOR |
| PRIVATE PRESS | BACK ROOM |
| COMMON TIME | AFTER TRADE |
| DUB PLATE SOCIAL | SLOW EXIT |
| ROOM SERVICE | RAW MATERIAL |

Pair it with a catalogue number. The format is the signal:
`SEC001` · `SEC-001` · `UNIT7.01` · `LC004` · `NS‑12‑03`

Most labels start at 001 and never explain it.

## Track titles

House track names are usually two words or fewer, present tense, no drama.

**Warmer, A-side energy**

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| Afternoon Tune | Soft Opening | First Hour |
| Plain Water | Housekeeping | Slow Exit |
| Still Early | Hold Music | Front Room |

**Deeper, B-side energy**

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| Last Record | Back Room | Four In |
| Night Porter | Quiet Part | After Trade |
| Low Ceiling | The Long Way | Lights Up |

**Extras that read as house**

- A version suffix: `Plain Water (Dub)` · `(Beats)` · `(Tool)` · `(Reprise)`
- A time suffix: `Four In (5AM Mix)`
- An initial: `For M.`
- A bare number: `Untitled 3`

# What actually has to be on it

This is the part people forget. A sleeve missing these reads as a mockup
rather than a record.

## Front

Often almost nothing. Legitimately: artist, title, nothing else. Some house
sleeves carry only the catalogue number.

## Back — the required furniture

1. **Artist and title**
2. **Catalogue number** — repeat it, it is how shops file it
3. **Tracklist with side letters** — `A1 A2 B1 B2`, and times if you have them
4. **Written and produced by** — your name or alias
5. **Mastered and cut by** — engineer name and studio. Cut is specific to
   vinyl and signals you actually pressed it
6. **Artwork and design by** — your credit, on your own project
7. **Publishing** — `Published by [label]` or `Unpublished`
8. **℗ and © line** — `℗ & © 2026 [label]`. ℗ is the recording, © is the
   artwork. Both, with the year
9. **The legal sentence** — *"Unauthorised copying, hiring, lending, public
   performance and broadcasting of this recording is prohibited."*
10. **Contact** — one line. Website, or an email, or an Instagram handle
11. **Barcode area** — leave 40 × 25 mm clear, lower right. Even if you never
    get one, the space being there is what makes it look real

## On the disc label

- Side letter, large
- Catalogue number
- **RPM** — `33⅓` or `45`. Not optional, people need to know
- Track titles for that side only
- Artist and title, small

## The details that make it convincing

- **Runout groove etching.** The blank ring between the last track and the
  label. Cutting engineers scratch a message there by hand. One short phrase,
  or the cat number, or initials. It is the most insider detail on a record
  and costs nothing to specify
- **"PROMO — NOT FOR RESALE"** stamp if you want the white-label look
- **A sticker on the sleeve** instead of printing the info — very common for
  minimal house, and it means the sleeve itself stays blank
- **BPM and key per track** on the back. DJs genuinely use this
- `PLAY LOUD` · `PLAY AT 33⅓` — one instruction, somewhere small

---

## One practical warning

Search any label name before you commit to it. Discogs, Bandcamp, Instagram,
and a trademark search. House label names are short and the good ones are
mostly taken — finding out after you have printed is expensive, and putting
work under someone else's label name in a portfolio causes real problems.
